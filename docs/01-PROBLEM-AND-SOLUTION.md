# Surge: Problem and Solution

## The problem

When every 911 dispatcher is busy, new callers wait. They hear ringing or a recording, and nobody knows what their emergency is until a human picks up. Calls are answered in the order they arrived, not by how serious they are.

This happens every day in understaffed centers, and it gets dangerous fast during big incidents:

- During Hurricane Harvey, Harris County 911 normally took 3,000 calls a day. During the storm it averaged 3,000 calls an hour, and one day the average wait to answer was 22 minutes.
- In a 2023 national survey, 36% of 774 dispatch centers reported vacancy rates above 30%.
- In Ingham County, Michigan, the 911 director reports running about 30% below target staffing.

## The solution

Surge replaces the hold queue, not the dispatcher.

1. **Always on.** Dispatchers work from a live map of their area with NASA hazard data (active fire detections) and every open call.
2. **When all dispatchers are busy, AI answers.** Within seconds, in any language: "911. All dispatchers are helping other callers. I'm an automated assistant and I'll get you to one as fast as possible. What's the address of your emergency?"
3. **It collects the essentials.** Location, what's happening, how many people, injuries, immediate danger, callback number. It stays on the line.
4. **It pins the call on the map and ranks it.** Our scoring rules set the priority and show the reason ("Priority 1: person not breathing"). Duplicate calls about the same incident are grouped.
5. **The dispatcher takes the most urgent call first.** One tap transfers the live call to them with the summary already on screen. The caller never repeats themselves.

## Who it's for

- **Callers:** answered in seconds instead of waiting.
- **Dispatchers:** see every waiting call, ranked, on one map.
- **Communities:** the most serious emergencies get help first.

## One line

When every dispatcher is busy, Surge answers, finds out what's wrong, and puts the most urgent call at the top of the map.
